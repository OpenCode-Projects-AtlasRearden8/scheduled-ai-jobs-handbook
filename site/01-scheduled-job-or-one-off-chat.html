01 · Scheduled job or one-off chat?

Before you automate anything, spend two minutes deciding whether it deserves a schedule at all. This is the judgment call the whole book rests on, and it's honestly just three questions.

The three questions

1. Would you run this again, on roughly the same clock?

If you'd prompt it today and prompt it again next Tuesday in mostly the same words — schedule it. If today's request is shaped around today's weird problem ("help me understand why my spreadsheet total is off"), keep it as a one-off chat.

2. Can the job be described once, in advance, as a fixed recipe?

A scheduled job has to survive with zero context from you. "Summarize everything I saved in notes.md over the last day" works because the inputs and the instruction are both knowable ahead of time. "Help me think through what I should do about David's email" isn't — it needs you in the loop.

3. Is a wrong answer cheap, or at least visible?

Every scheduled job will someday produce something confidently wrong. model misreads your notes, or the output is bland when you wanted sharp. If the output is a draft digest you'll skim for two minutes — fine, wrong-ish for a day costs nothing. If the output directly sends money, sends email on your behalf, or feeds medical decisions — don't schedule it raw. This book is education, never medical advice, and that goes double for anything that runs while you're asleep.

A good first scheduled job

Pick something where the failure mode is "bland summary, deleted tomorrow." The morning digest (Chapter 5) is the classic. Yours might be a weekly review of your todo file, or a Friday summary of your eBay activity file. Whatever it is, it should be: same input file, same instruction, same clock, and human-skimmed output.
